
## 14. The BSP and Executive Departments and Agencies

How the BSP coordinates with executive departments and government agencies while maintaining a clear distinction between central-banking functions and executive administration

**Why This Relationship Matters**

The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) operates within a government system containing many specialized institutions.

Executive departments and agencies perform functions involving areas such as fiscal administration, budgeting, economic planning, trade, statistics, agriculture, infrastructure, and other public responsibilities.

The BSP's responsibilities sometimes intersect with these functions because economic and financial conditions are interconnected.

For example, a government spending decision can influence economic activity. A change in international trade can affect prices. A supply disruption can contribute to inflation. A financial-market disturbance can require several institutions to respond.

The BSP therefore cannot operate in complete isolation from the rest of government.

At the same time, coordination does not mean that the BSP becomes an ordinary executive agency or that another agency acquires the BSP's monetary authority.

The central principle is:

> **The BSP and executive departments can work together on matters of common concern while remaining separate institutions with different legal mandates.**

---

**1. What Are Executive Departments and Agencies?**

Executive departments and agencies are public institutions established to perform particular governmental functions.

They form part of the country's administrative machinery and are responsible for implementing or administering laws and government programs within their respective mandates.

Examples include institutions responsible for:

- finance;
- budgeting;
- economic planning;
- trade and industry;
- agriculture;
- infrastructure;
- statistics; and
- other areas of public administration.

Their responsibilities can affect economic conditions even when those responsibilities are not monetary in nature.

---

**2. Why Does the BSP Need to Coordinate With Them?**

The economy is interconnected.

No single institution controls every factor affecting economic activity.

Consider inflation.

Prices can be affected by:

- monetary conditions;
- food supply;
- energy costs;
- transportation;
- imports;
- exchange-rate movements;
- international commodity prices; and
- domestic production.

Some of these factors fall directly within the BSP's responsibilities.

Others fall primarily within the responsibilities of different government institutions.

Therefore, effective economic governance requires institutions to understand how their respective actions interact.

---

**3. What Does Coordination Actually Mean?**

Coordination means **working with another institution when responsibilities intersect**.

It can involve:

- consultation;
- information-sharing;
- technical discussions;
- joint assessments;
- participation in interagency bodies;
- coordinated responses; and
- clarification of institutional responsibilities.

Coordination does not necessarily mean agreement.

It also does not mean that one institution becomes subordinate to another.

| Coordination | Control |
|---|---|
| Consultation | Command |
| Information-sharing | Unauthorized direction |
| Cooperation | Subordination |
| Joint assessment | Taking over another mandate |
| Respect for separate authority | Eliminating institutional boundaries |

Therefore:

> **Coordination is cooperation between institutions; control is the exercise of authority over another institution.**

---

**4. Why Can Different Agencies Work on the Same Economic Problem?**

Because one problem can have several dimensions.

Imagine that food prices rise sharply.

One agency may investigate agricultural supply.

Another may examine import conditions.

Another may study transportation or logistics.

The BSP may assess whether the price increase is becoming a broader and more persistent inflation problem.

They are not necessarily doing the same thing.

They are examining **different dimensions of the same economic event**.

**Think of it like a large puzzle.**

Each institution has a different group of pieces.

Coordination allows the government to understand the whole picture without requiring every institution to possess every piece.

---

**5. What Is the BSP's Distinct Role?**

The BSP's role is determined by its legal mandate as the country's central monetary authority and central bank.

Its responsibilities include monetary and financial functions assigned to it by law.

Executive departments have different responsibilities.

Therefore, the BSP's participation in an interagency discussion does not transform that discussion into a BSP-controlled activity.

Likewise, participation by an executive department in an economic discussion does not give that department authority over BSP decisions.

---

**6. The BSP and the Department of Finance**

The Department of Finance (DOF) is particularly important because fiscal conditions and monetary conditions interact.

The DOF is responsible for functions involving government finances and fiscal policy within its legal mandate.

The BSP performs monetary and central-banking functions within its own legal mandate.

These areas can influence each other.

**Example**

Suppose the government increases public spending.

The DOF may examine:

- government revenue;
- financing;
- borrowing;
- public debt; and
- fiscal sustainability.

The BSP may examine:

- inflationary effects;
- monetary conditions;
- liquidity;
- financial-market conditions.

The same government action can therefore be relevant to both institutions without giving either institution the other's responsibilities.

---

**7. Why Must Fiscal and Monetary Functions Remain Distinct?**

Fiscal policy and monetary policy are related, but they are not interchangeable.

**Fiscal policy** primarily concerns government taxation, spending, borrowing, and related fiscal decisions.

**Monetary policy** concerns monetary conditions managed through the central bank's legally assigned authority.

A government may have fiscal objectives.

The BSP must perform its monetary responsibilities according to its own mandate.

**Think of it like two specialized control systems.**

The fiscal authorities operate one system.

The BSP operates another.

The systems interact, but they are not the same system.

---

**8. The BSP and the Department of Budget and Management**

The Department of Budget and Management (DBM) has responsibilities concerning the national government's budgetary administration.

Budget decisions can affect economic activity and therefore may be relevant to the BSP's analysis.

However, the BSP does not administer the national government's budget simply because budgetary conditions can affect monetary conditions.

**Scenario:**

Suppose government expenditure is projected to increase significantly.

The DBM may focus on budget implementation and expenditure management.

The BSP may assess the broader economic and monetary consequences.

Both institutions can examine the same development from different institutional perspectives.

---

**9. The BSP and Economic Planning Institutions**

Economic planning institutions consider matters such as:

- economic growth;
- investment;
- development;
- productivity;
- employment; and
- long-term national objectives.

These matters are relevant to the BSP because economic conditions influence monetary and financial conditions.

However, an economic-development plan does not automatically determine the BSP's monetary decisions.

**Example**

Suppose the government wants to encourage greater investment.

Planning authorities may examine how investment can increase production and productivity.

The BSP may consider whether changing economic conditions affect inflation and financial stability.

Both perspectives can be valid simultaneously.

---

**10. The BSP and Trade-Related Agencies**

Trade developments can affect:

- imports;
- exports;
- prices;
- foreign exchange;
- production;
- supply chains; and
- economic activity.

The BSP therefore has legitimate reasons to monitor trade developments.

But monitoring their monetary consequences does not make the BSP the country's trade administrator.

**Scenario:**

Suppose international food prices rise.

Trade authorities may examine import measures.

The BSP may evaluate the implications for inflation.

The trade authority addresses the **trade dimension**.

The BSP addresses the **monetary dimension**.

---

**11. The BSP and Government Statistical Institutions**

Reliable information is essential for economic decision-making.

Government statistical institutions provide data concerning areas such as:

- prices;
- employment;
- production;
- trade;
- national accounts; and
- other economic indicators.

The BSP can use these data for economic and monetary analysis.

But using another institution's information does not give the BSP authority over that institution.

Similarly, producing official statistics does not give a statistical institution authority over BSP monetary decisions.

> **Information may be shared without authority being transferred.**

---

**12. Why Is Information-Sharing Important?**

Different institutions possess different information.

The BSP has specialized information concerning areas within its central-banking responsibilities.

Other agencies possess information concerning:

- fiscal conditions;
- production;
- trade;
- employment;
- government programs;
- infrastructure; and
- other economic developments.

Combining relevant information can produce a more complete understanding of economic conditions.

However, information-sharing must remain consistent with applicable:

- confidentiality requirements;
- legal restrictions;
- data-protection requirements; and
- institutional procedures.

---

**13. Does Information-Sharing Make One Institution Subordinate?**

No.

Suppose an executive agency provides information to the BSP.

The agency has not thereby gained authority to determine BSP policy.

Likewise, if the BSP lawfully provides information to another government institution, the BSP does not thereby become subordinate to that institution.

The distinction is straightforward:

> **Sharing information is not the same as transferring decision-making authority.**

---

**14. What Happens When Several Agencies Face the Same Problem?**

Some problems require coordinated government attention.

Consider a severe economic shock.

It might simultaneously affect:

- prices;
- employment;
- government finances;
- trade;
- businesses;
- banks;
- financial markets.

Different institutions may therefore need to respond to different parts of the problem.

The BSP addresses matters within its mandate.

Other agencies address matters within theirs.

This is **division of responsibility**, not unnecessary duplication.

---

**15. Why Are Interagency Mechanisms Useful?**

Some economic and financial problems cross institutional boundaries.

Interagency committees, councils, working groups, and other coordination mechanisms can provide a forum for institutions to:

- exchange information;
- identify risks;
- coordinate responses;
- clarify responsibilities; and
- develop appropriate approaches.

These mechanisms are useful precisely because no single institution necessarily possesses all the information or authority relevant to a complex problem.

---

**16. Financial Stability as an Example**

Financial stability is an especially clear example of why institutional coordination matters.

The financial system involves:

- banks;
- other financial institutions;
- payment systems;
- markets;
- businesses;
- households; and
- government.

A serious problem in one part of the system can affect others.

The BSP therefore participates in appropriate financial-stability coordination with other relevant institutions.

The **Financial Stability Coordination Council (FSCC)** provides an example of an interagency framework for cooperation on systemic financial risks.

The lesson is important:

> **Coordination can strengthen system-wide financial oversight without eliminating the separate mandates of participating institutions.**

---

**17. What If the BSP and Another Agency Disagree?**

Disagreement does not automatically mean that coordination has failed.

Different institutions may legitimately reach different conclusions because they have different mandates.

**Scenario:**

Suppose an economic agency favors accelerating a particular program because it expects stronger economic growth.

The BSP may identify inflationary or financial risks associated with the same program.

The disagreement reflects different institutional perspectives.

The appropriate response is not automatically to eliminate one institution's judgment.

Instead, institutions should explain their reasoning and act within their respective legal mandates.

---

**18. Does Coordination Require Agreement?**

No.

Coordination means that institutions communicate and understand how their actions interact.

It does not necessarily require identical conclusions.

For example, two agencies may agree that a problem exists while disagreeing about the appropriate response.

They can still coordinate by:

- sharing information;
- explaining their assessments;
- identifying areas of agreement;
- identifying areas of disagreement; and
- acting within their respective authority.

Therefore:

> **Effective coordination can exist even when institutional decisions differ.**

---

**19. Can Executive Agencies Direct the BSP?**

An executive department or agency cannot simply assume the BSP's independent statutory functions merely because it is part of the Executive Branch.

The BSP's authority is established by its constitutional and statutory framework.

Therefore, another agency cannot create authority over the BSP simply by asserting that coordination requires it.

**Example**

If an executive agency disagrees with a BSP regulatory or monetary decision, disagreement by itself does not give that agency power to replace the BSP's decision.

Any intervention must have an appropriate legal basis.

---

**20. Can the BSP Direct Executive Agencies?**

The principle also applies to the BSP.

The BSP cannot simply assume responsibilities assigned by law to executive departments and agencies.

It does not become the general administrator of:

- agriculture;
- infrastructure;
- taxation;
- national budgeting;
- trade;
- social programs; or
- other executive functions merely because those areas affect the economy.

The BSP may analyze their economic consequences.

It may coordinate with the responsible agencies.

But **economic relevance does not automatically create legal authority**.

---

**21. Why Must Each Institution Know Its Own Mandate?**

Clear mandates prevent institutional confusion.

If every institution believed it could intervene in every economic matter, responsibility would become unclear.

A better approach is:

**IDENTIFY THE ISSUE**

↓

**IDENTIFY EACH INSTITUTION'S RESPONSIBILITY**

↓

**COORDINATE WHERE RESPONSIBILITIES INTERSECT**

↓

**EACH INSTITUTION ACTS WITHIN ITS AUTHORITY**

This makes responsibility easier to understand and accountability easier to establish.

---

**22. What Is Coordination Without Subordination?**

This is the central institutional concept of the chapter.

**Coordination** means:

> "Our responsibilities intersect, so we need to work together."

**Subordination** means:

> "Your institution is subject to my institution's direction."

The BSP can coordinate extensively with executive departments without automatically becoming subordinate to them.

**Think of it like specialized teams.**

An engineering team and a medical team may work together during a disaster.

Cooperation does not make one team a branch of the other.

Similarly, cooperation between the BSP and executive agencies does not erase their separate institutional responsibilities.

---

**23. Why Is Excessive Separation Also a Problem?**

Institutional independence does not mean institutional isolation.

If agencies refuse to communicate, they may fail to recognize how their decisions affect one another.

For example:

- fiscal developments can affect monetary conditions;
- trade developments can affect prices;
- supply disruptions can affect inflation;
- financial conditions can affect economic activity.

Therefore:

> **Independence without appropriate communication can weaken government coordination.**

---

**24. Why Is Excessive Integration Also a Problem?**

The opposite extreme can also be harmful.

If every institution is required to follow a single administrative direction regardless of its specialized mandate, institutional specialization becomes weakened.

For the BSP, excessive integration could blur the distinction between:

- monetary authority; and
- ordinary executive administration.

Therefore:

> **Coordination should connect institutions without absorbing one institution into another.**

---

**25. What Is the Proper Balance?**

The appropriate relationship rests on three principles:

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Distinct mandates** | Each institution has its own legal responsibilities |
| **Coordination** | Institutions cooperate when their responsibilities intersect |
| **Legal boundaries** | Neither institution assumes powers belonging to another |

In simple form:

**SEPARATE MANDATES**

↓

**COORDINATION**

↓

**RESPECT FOR BOUNDARIES**

↓

**EFFECTIVE GOVERNANCE**

---

**26. Scenario: Large Government Infrastructure Spending**

Imagine the government begins a major infrastructure program.

It may involve:

- government expenditure;
- borrowing;
- imports;
- employment;
- private investment;
- construction activity.

Several institutions may therefore become involved.

Fiscal authorities may address financing.

Planning institutions may assess development effects.

Trade authorities may consider import-related issues.

The BSP may evaluate monetary and financial consequences.

No contradiction exists in having multiple institutions involved because **the same program can have multiple institutional dimensions**.

---

**27. Scenario: Sudden Food-Price Increase**

Suppose food prices rise sharply after a major supply disruption.

Agricultural authorities may examine production.

Trade authorities may examine import conditions.

Other agencies may address logistics and supply chains.

The BSP may assess whether the price increase is temporary or becoming broader and more persistent inflation.

The important lesson is:

> **The BSP can respond to the monetary consequences of an economic problem without being responsible for every underlying cause of that problem.**

---

**28. Scenario: Financial-Market Disruption**

Suppose financial markets experience severe volatility.

The BSP may monitor conditions affecting:

- banks;
- liquidity;
- payments;
- financial stability.

Other institutions may examine issues involving:

- securities markets;
- insurance;
- fiscal consequences;
- broader economic effects.

The situation may require coordinated government attention.

But each institution continues to exercise its own legally assigned responsibilities.

---

**29. Scenario: Natural Disaster**

Imagine a major disaster disrupts economic activity.

Executive agencies may provide:

- emergency assistance;
- infrastructure restoration;
- food support;
- transportation assistance;
- fiscal assistance.

The BSP may address financial and monetary issues falling within its mandate, including matters affecting financial institutions and payment systems where relevant.

The BSP therefore participates in the broader national response **without becoming the administrator of the entire disaster response**.

---

**30. Why Does This Matter to Ordinary Filipinos?**

Institutional coordination has practical consequences.

A Filipino household does not experience "monetary policy" and "fiscal policy" as isolated academic concepts.

People experience:

- food prices;
- loan costs;
- employment;
- business conditions;
- government services;
- financial access;
- payment systems.

Different institutions can influence different parts of these experiences.

Understanding their respective responsibilities helps the public determine **which institution is responsible for what**.

---

**31. Why Should the BSP Not Be Blamed for Every Economic Problem?**

Economic outcomes have many causes.

They may include:

- international commodity prices;
- natural disasters;
- supply disruptions;
- agricultural conditions;
- global financial developments;
- trade conditions;
- fiscal decisions;
- productivity;
- infrastructure; and
- domestic demand.

The BSP has important responsibilities, but it does not control every economic variable.

Therefore:

> **Economic importance does not mean universal economic control.**

---

**32. Why Should Executive Agencies Understand the BSP?**

Executive agencies need to understand the BSP's specialized role so that coordination does not become institutional confusion.

They should recognize that:

- the BSP has legally defined responsibilities;
- monetary authority is specialized;
- central-banking decisions are not ordinary administrative decisions;
- cooperation does not automatically create authority over the BSP.

This understanding makes interagency cooperation more effective.

---

**33. Why Should the BSP Understand Executive Agencies?**

The relationship is mutual.

The BSP also needs to understand the mandates of executive departments and agencies.

For example, the BSP should recognize that:

- fiscal administration belongs to responsible fiscal institutions;
- budget administration belongs to responsible budget authorities;
- trade administration belongs to responsible trade authorities;
- agricultural programs belong to responsible agricultural authorities.

The BSP can evaluate the economic consequences of these policies without assuming responsibility for administering them.

---

**34. What Happens When Mandates Intersect?**

Sometimes a single issue legitimately falls within several institutional mandates.

For example, a financial problem might involve:

- monetary conditions;
- banking;
- securities;
- insurance;
- government finances;
- consumer protection.

The proper approach is to determine:

1. what the issue is;
2. which institutions have relevant legal responsibilities;
3. where those responsibilities intersect;
4. what information should be shared;
5. what coordination is appropriate; and
6. which institution retains authority over its own function.

This turns potential institutional conflict into structured cooperation.

---

**35. Can Coordination Create New Legal Authority?**

Not by itself.

Suppose two agencies agree that a particular action would be useful.

Their agreement does not automatically give either institution a power that the law has not granted.

Each institution must still identify its legal authority.

Therefore:

> **Coordination can improve the exercise of lawful authority; it cannot manufacture authority.**

---

**36. Why Is This Different From an Ordinary Chain of Command?**

Ordinary executive administration commonly operates through hierarchical relationships.

An office may report to a department.

A bureau may operate under a department.

Officials may exercise administrative authority within that structure.

The BSP's institutional position is different because its central-banking independence is established within its constitutional and statutory framework.

Therefore, coordination with an executive department should not be mistaken for placement within that department's ordinary administrative hierarchy.

---

**37. What Is the Risk of Confusing Coordination With Control?**

If coordination is mistaken for control, agencies may begin assuming authority they do not possess.

This can produce:

- institutional conflict;
- unclear accountability;
- duplication;
- unauthorized intervention; and
- weakened specialization.

Clear distinctions therefore protect both the BSP and the other institutions.

---

**38. What Is the Risk of Confusing Independence With Isolation?**

The opposite misunderstanding can also cause problems.

If independence is interpreted as refusing all cooperation, institutions may fail to exchange information or coordinate during situations that affect the entire financial or economic system.

The correct principle is:

> **Institutional independence should protect lawful decision-making, not prevent necessary institutional communication.**

---

**39. What Does Good Interagency Coordination Look Like?**

Good coordination means:

- each institution understands its mandate;
- relevant information is exchanged lawfully;
- institutions communicate professionally;
- disagreements can be discussed;
- responsibilities remain identifiable;
- coordinated responses are possible;
- no institution assumes another's authority; and
- each institution remains accountable for its own decisions.

This is more than simply saying:

> "Government agencies should cooperate."

The important question is **whether they cooperate while preserving the legal structure of their respective responsibilities**.

---

**40. What Does Poor Coordination Look Like?**

Poor coordination can take several forms.

**Too little coordination**

Institutions fail to communicate and overlook interactions between their policies.

**Too much institutional control**

One institution attempts to dictate another institution's decisions without lawful authority.

**Mandate confusion**

Institutions become unclear about who is responsible for what.

**Information failure**

Important information is not shared appropriately.

**Accountability failure**

It becomes impossible to determine which institution made a particular decision.

Effective governance requires avoiding all four problems.

---

**41. Why Does Professional Respect Matter?**

Institutional cooperation depends not only on formal rules but also on professional conduct.

Institutions should be able to:

- disagree without hostility;
- exchange technical assessments;
- recognize legitimate differences;
- respect confidential information;
- acknowledge institutional boundaries; and
- communicate clearly.

Professional disagreement is healthier than institutional competition for authority.

---

**42. Why Is Accountability Still Important?**

Coordination does not eliminate responsibility.

If the BSP makes a decision within its mandate, the BSP remains responsible for that institutional action.

If an executive department makes a decision within its mandate, that institution remains responsible for its action.

Coordination should therefore make responsibilities **clearer**, not blur them.

---

**43. Why Is Transparency Relevant?**

Appropriate transparency helps the public understand why several government institutions may be involved in the same economic issue.

For example, when inflation rises, the public may reasonably ask:

> "Why isn't the BSP simply fixing the problem?"

Understanding institutional responsibilities provides the answer.

The BSP may address monetary aspects.

Other agencies may address supply, fiscal, trade, agricultural, or administrative aspects.

Transparency helps explain these distinctions.

---

**44. Why Is This Relationship Important During Normal Times?**

Interagency coordination should not begin only after a crisis occurs.

Regular professional communication can help institutions:

- understand economic developments;
- identify emerging risks;
- anticipate policy interactions;
- improve information quality; and
- prepare for potential disruptions.

Good coordination is therefore both **preventive and responsive**.

---

**45. Why Is This Relationship Especially Important During Crises?**

During a crisis, events can move faster than ordinary institutional processes.

A financial disruption may quickly affect:

- banks;
- businesses;
- households;
- markets;
- payments;
- government finances.

Institutions may therefore need to communicate rapidly.

But urgency does not automatically eliminate legal boundaries.

Even during a crisis:

> **Coordination must remain connected to lawful institutional authority.**

---

**46. The Three Questions for Any BSP–Agency Relationship**

Whenever the BSP interacts with an executive department or agency, three questions provide a useful framework.

**1. What is the problem?**

What economic, financial, regulatory, or administrative issue requires attention?

**2. Who is legally responsible?**

Which institution has authority over each part of the problem?

**3. What coordination is appropriate?**

Should the institutions:

- exchange information;
- consult;
- coordinate;
- participate in an interagency mechanism; or
- act independently?

These questions help prevent both unnecessary separation and inappropriate intervention.

---

**47. The Broader Governance Lesson**

The BSP's relationship with executive departments illustrates a broader principle:

> **A government can have many specialized institutions without requiring them to operate as isolated entities.**

Specialization provides expertise.

Coordination provides coherence.

Legal boundaries provide accountability.

Together, these elements allow different institutions to contribute to common national objectives while retaining their distinct responsibilities.

---

**48. The Proper Institutional Model**

The relationship can be summarized simply:

**BSP**

↓

**SPECIALIZED CENTRAL-BANK FUNCTIONS**

↕

**INTERAGENCY COORDINATION**

↕

**EXECUTIVE DEPARTMENTS AND AGENCIES**

↓

**THEIR OWN SPECIALIZED FUNCTIONS**

The central idea is that the institutions **interact**, but their legal mandates do not automatically merge.

---

---

**50. What Is the Proper Relationship Between the BSP and Executive Departments and Agencies?**

The complete answer is:

> **The BSP and executive departments and agencies are separate public institutions with different legally assigned responsibilities. Their work inevitably intersects because monetary, fiscal, financial, economic, administrative, and social conditions are interconnected. Consequently, they may communicate, exchange relevant information, consult, and coordinate when their responsibilities intersect, including through appropriate interagency mechanisms. Such coordination does not by itself create a hierarchical relationship or transfer one institution's legal authority to another. The BSP remains responsible for performing its own central-banking functions, while executive departments and agencies remain responsible for their respective governmental functions. Each institution must therefore cooperate where necessary while remaining within the authority assigned to it by law.**

The relationship can ultimately be expressed in four words:

> **Different mandates. Coordinated action.**

---

**Conclusion**

The BSP's relationship with executive departments and agencies is neither one of isolation nor ordinary administrative subordination.

It is a relationship of **institutional cooperation among bodies with different responsibilities**.

The BSP operates in an economy affected by fiscal decisions, trade, production, government programs, international developments, financial markets, and countless other factors.

It therefore needs information and coordination from other institutions.

At the same time, executive departments and agencies can benefit from the BSP's specialized knowledge concerning monetary and financial conditions.

But cooperation must not erase institutional boundaries.

The DOF remains responsible for its fiscal functions.

The DBM remains responsible for its budgetary functions.

Economic planning institutions remain responsible for their planning functions.

Trade authorities remain responsible for their trade-related functions.

Statistical institutions remain responsible for producing official statistics within their mandates.

And the BSP remains responsible for the central-banking functions assigned to it by law.

The governing principle is therefore:

**SEPARATE MANDATES**

↓

**SHARED INFORMATION**

↓

**COORDINATION**

↓

**RESPECT FOR LEGAL AUTHORITY**

↓

**CLEAR RESPONSIBILITY**

↓

**EFFECTIVE GOVERNANCE**

The most important lesson is simple:

> **The BSP does not have to stand apart from government in order to remain institutionally distinct from executive administration.**

It can communicate without becoming subordinate.

It can cooperate without surrendering its institutional identity.

It can coordinate without acquiring the functions of other agencies.

And executive agencies can work with the BSP without taking over responsibilities legally assigned to the central bank.

Ultimately, effective governance requires both **specialization and cooperation**.

**Specialization** ensures that institutions develop the expertise required for their responsibilities.

**Cooperation** ensures that those specialized responsibilities do not operate blindly toward one another.

**Legal boundaries** ensure that cooperation does not become unauthorized control.

Therefore, the proper relationship between the BSP and executive departments and agencies is:

> **Separate institutions, distinct mandates, lawful coordination, and shared responsibility for effective public governance.**
